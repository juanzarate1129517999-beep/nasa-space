Abstract

Artemis lunar-surface habitats and pressurized rovers will run at 30% oxygen or more and 10.2 psi or less. Skylab Phoenix answers, from NASA's own flight data, what happens to fire in that atmosphere and where that data runs out.

We built a data-engineering pipeline that downloads, cleans and unifies the experimental tables of the 24 Combustion Science investigations in NASA's PSI repository. The result is 1,955 records in one schema, 688 with a labeled outcome. We corrected 191 errors in the source tables and logged every correction. On top of that data we profile and rank each investigation per habitat and train interpretable models with explicit uncertainty.

The main model predicts whether a flame puts itself out. Trained on 261 droplet fires from the FLEX experiment on the ISS and validated by holding out whole test days, it reaches AUC 0.88 and Brier 0.125, ahead of gradient boosting (AUC 0.84) and an oxygen-only baseline (0.80). According to the model, the chance of self-extinction falls from 72% in a spacecraft cabin to 6% in the lunar base (90% interval 2%–14%). Only 37 of 1,613 flight records lie near that atmosphere, and none is a solid material. For the spacesuit (100% O₂, 4.3 psi) there is no test at all, and the system refuses to predict.

Active learning recommends the next experiment: CO₂ suppression (15%–30% CO₂) at 25%–27% O₂ and 0.55–0.70 atm, where the model is least certain. An SI layer writes plain-language briefings for every habitat and investigation; code publishes a briefing only if every sentence cites the data and every number is verified (33 of 33 pass). Everything is presented in a bilingual (English and Spanish) website where the models run live in the browser. It includes an immersive 3D story from NASA's Artemis plan to the lunar south pole, a Fire Lab where a droplet burns by the d² law with burning rates measured in FLEX, a cross-filtered dashboard over all 1,955 records, and a fire audit of a three-module lunar base in which every number is labelled by where it comes from.

The message in one sentence: with more oxygen, fire in microgravity stops putting itself out, and NASA has almost no flight data in the atmosphere where astronauts will live on the Moon. Skylab Phoenix quantifies it, shows it and says what to test next.

Introduction

Fire is one of the most serious risks of crewed spaceflight, and in microgravity it behaves differently than on Earth.

The history of spaceflight is a reminder: the Apollo 1 fire in a pure-oxygen cabin (1967), the oxygen-tank explosion on Apollo 13 (1970) and the oxygen-generator fire on the Mir station (1997). During Artemis II, a smoke alarm sounded about 80,000 miles from Earth. NASA's strategy has three layers: prevention (controlling ignition sources and choosing materials by their flammability limits), detection and suppression.

In microgravity there is no buoyancy: hot gas does not rise, a candle flame becomes a blue sphere, and oxygen arrives only by diffusion or ventilation. That changes flammability limits, flame spread and extinction. NASA has measured this for decades on the ISS and on cargo spacecraft and publishes the results in the Physical Sciences Informatics (PSI) repository. The problem the challenge poses is that this volume of data is hard to find, compare and interpret.

The urgency is concrete. To shorten pre-breathing before spacewalks, the lunar base and pressurized rovers will have more oxygen and less pressure than Earth's atmosphere. That combination makes materials much more flammable, and it is exactly where flight data is scarce. Skylab Phoenix sets out to:

  1. Turn PSI's scattered data into a clean, unified and traceable dataset.
  2. Extract quantitative knowledge from it with validated models that are honest about their uncertainty.
  3. Present it in a dashboard, with SI briefings, that any researcher, engineer or student can use to make decisions.

Research question

What does NASA's flight data say about fire behaviour in the atmosphere planned for the lunar base (≥ 30% O₂, ≤ 10.2 psi), how certain is it, and which experiment is missing to answer what is unknown today?

The challenge page gives several hints that we used as a guide. The μg tests with the most oxygen are those of ACME CFI-G (up to 85% O₂ at the burner). PSI has no partial-gravity data yet (it will come from LUCI and FM2). Microgravity and 1 g results can bound intermediate gravity. And ACME's BRE experiment studied material flammability with gases that emulate solids.

Target users

Professors, graduate and undergraduate students, researchers and, above all, aerospace engineers who design habitats and select materials. The dashboard offers a guided view for each of these audiences.

Conceptual framework: fire in microgravity

Without buoyancy, fire depends on transport by diffusion and ventilation. Gravity, airflow, oxygen and pressure therefore interact in ways a test on Earth does not capture.

Key concepts

  • Flammability limit. The least favourable combination of conditions (O₂, pressure, flow) in which a material sustains a flame. Microgravity adds a low-flow limit: with little flow, the flame goes out for lack of oxygen and through radiative losses.
  • Self-extinction. The flame goes out on its own before consuming the fuel. It is the target of our main model: if a fire does not go out on its own, it must be detected and suppressed.
  • Concurrent vs opposed flow. When air moves in the direction of flame spread, it preheats the fuel and speeds the flame up. When it moves against it, it slows it down.
  • Normal vs inverse flame. In a normal flame, fuel leaves the burner into an oxidizing ambient. In an inverse flame it is the other way round: O₂ leaves the burner into a fuel-laden ambient.
  • Adiabatic flame temperature (Tad). The theoretical maximum temperature of combustion without losses, used as an indicator of how close a flame is to its limit.
  • Cool flames. Low-temperature, nearly invisible combustion seen with fuels such as butane. A distinct risk: an optical detector may miss it.
  • Diluents. CO₂ and helium dilute oxygen and change heat transfer. FLEX tested them as suppressants.

Data: source, scope and inventory

We use a single data source, the one the challenge names: NASA's PSI repository (CC0 license). Of its 133 results for “combustion”, 24 belong to Combustion Science: 19 flight experiments and 5 ground investigations.

The other 109 investigations (materials, fluids, biophysics) only mention the word and are discarded. Everything is retrieved without login from PSI's public API: metadata (objective, hypothesis, approach, team, publications, Earth benefit) and experimental tables in CSV or Excel. Videos and raw images weigh hundreds of GB and are not needed. The challenge's other resources (Researcher's Guide, NASA Combustion Science, Apollo, Mir and the spinoffs) served as context, not for training models.

Not every investigation publishes a test table: 18 of the 24 do. The five ground studies and FLEX-2 provide descriptions, publications and data products but no test-by-test table, so they enter the profiles and the ranking but contribute no records. Layers

  • Bronze (scripts/fetch_psi*.py): downloads the catalog and the lightweight files of each investigation (≈ 55 MB).
  • Silver (pipeline/silver.py): one function per experiment maps each table to the common schema (Annex A). It converts mmHg and bar to atm and percentages to mole fractions, normalizes labels (“Extinction”, “SE”/“FT”, yes/Yes/flash) and extracts the outcome from the operator's notes in BASS. It also separates flow sweeps and flags O₂ and pressure when they are not reported.
  • Gold (pipeline/gold.py, pipeline/investigations.py): unified test table, profile of each investigation, similarity, per-habitat ranking, findings with evidence scores and web export.

Quality rules

The pipeline fails on duplicate ids, mole fractions outside [0, 1] or pressures outside [0.1, 6] atm. It also applies correction rules: physically impossible values, mole fractions that do not sum to 1, spreadsheet auto-fill errors, duplicate rows and mis-formatted percentages. The full log is in Annex C.

Examples: a 0 mmHg pressure in FLEX (excluded from the models), “3400%” and “2100%” in ACME BRE (34% and 21%), 8 identical duplicate rows in CFI, a missing decimal point in Saffire-II (“037 mm” → 0.37 mm) and 19 lowercase “yes/no” labels in CFI-G. None was fixed silently: all can be audited.

A second pass compared tables with each other and with NASA's own files. It found calendar dates that disagree with the GMT day of year in BASS, one as-run number used for two tests on different days, a BASS-II GMT day contradicted by the name of NASA's raw video for that test, Saffire thicknesses written in centimetres instead of millimetres, sample length and width swapped, camera file names that do not match the sample, and a Greek μ exported as “?”. In BASS-II gas data, final O₂ readings above the initial value and CO readings that fall during a burn are physically impossible in the sealed glovebox and are excluded. The quality log now holds 191 entries.

Summaries, ranking and similarity of investigations

Each investigation has a profile generated from its data and a relevance score per habitat. The score is a visible formula, not a black box.

Automatic profile

For each experimental table, the pipeline classifies every column as varied (with its range), fixed (with its value) or measured (an outcome). This directly answers the challenge's question: “how did the tests within one investigation differ, and how were they alike?”. The profile adds the objective, hypothesis, team, hardware, conditions covered, publications with DOI and the Earth benefit reported by PSI.

Ranking per habitat

Relevance = 100 × ( 0.5 · proximity + 0.2 · evidence + 0.3 · directness )
  • Proximity: 1 − e−n/8, where n is the effective number of tests near the habitat atmosphere, with a Gaussian kernel of 3 points of O₂ and 30% of pressure. It counts half when the O₂ is at the burner or the conditions were assumed.
  • Evidence: log(1 + tests with an outcome), normalized to 300.
  • Directness: 1.0 if the challenge page names it as core (BASS, BASS-II, Saffire, SAME, SAME-R, FLEX); 0.75 if it mentions it (BRE, DAFT, CFI-G, E-FIELD); 0.5 for other flight investigations; 0.35 for ground ones.

Machine learning

With hundreds of tests rather than millions, we chose small, interpretable, calibrated models. We compare them with baselines and validate them without letting tests from the same day leak between training and testing.

8.1 Problem formulation

Target: does the flame put itself out? (FLEX: “Extinction” = 1; “Disruption” or “Completion” = 0). Base rate: 68.6%. We used 261 of the 274 tests: 13 were excluded, one with an invalid pressure and twelve without an initial diameter.

Table 5. Inputs (six, each with a physical meaning).

InputWhy
O₂ mole fractionControls reaction rate and flame temperature
ln(pressure)Pressure changes diffusion and soot radiation
CO₂ and helium fractionsSuppressing diluents: heat capacity (CO₂) and diffusivity (He)
Initial droplet diameterLarge droplets make large flames, with more radiative loss
Fuel (heptane / methanol)Heptane soots more and burns differently than methanol

8.2 Validation design

  • 5-fold GroupKFold with 80 test days as groups: each whole day is either in training or in testing, never both.
  • Out-of-sample metrics: AUC (ranking ability), Brier and log loss (quality of the probabilities) and accuracy.
  • Comparison with gradient boosting (same inputs) and two baselines: oxygen only, and a constant base rate.
  • Uncertainty: 200 bootstrap models resampling whole days. Each prediction reports the mean and the 90% interval.

8.6 “Out-of-data” guard

A model always returns a number, even when the question is far from what was tested. To prevent that, we compute the mean distance to the 5 nearest tests in the standardized input space. The limit is the 95th percentile of that distance among the tests themselves (1.33). Up to 1× the limit: within the data. Between 1× and 2×: extrapolation (shown with a warning). Beyond 2×: no prediction. In addition, if any single input leaves the range flown in FLEX (for example, a pressure below every test), the prediction is flagged as an extrapolation even when the distance is small: this is the case of the exploration atmosphere (34% O₂, 8.2 psi). The lunar base sits at 0.80 (within the data). The spacesuit sits at 14.3, about ten times the limit, so Skylab Phoenix gives no number and reports a gap.

8.7 Hypothesis test: a limiting flame temperature? (ACME CFI-G)

For normal flames, self-extinction becomes more likely as flame temperature falls. The 50% point (≈ 2,844 K; 90% CI 2,740–3,135 K) lies just above the hottest flame tested (2,730 K), so it is an extrapolation. For inverse flames, no single temperature explains extinction. Conclusion: flame temperature is not a universal criterion, and reporting this negative result is part of the project's scientific honesty.

8.8 Fire Lab: the d² law from flight data

The model says how often a fire goes out; the Fire Lab shows how it burns. A burning droplet in microgravity follows the classic d² law: the square of its diameter falls linearly in time, d² = d₀² − K·t, where K is the burning-rate constant. FLEX measured K for 257 of its tests (median 0.46 mm²/s for methanol and 0.54 mm²/s for heptane, range 0.05–1.06), and the diameter at which the visible flame went out.

  • For any scenario the Fire Lab takes the five FLEX tests closest in the model's standardized inputs and uses their distance-weighted K.
  • When a replay goes out on its own (drawn with the model's probability), it does so at the extinction ratio dext/d₀ of the closest tests that self-extinguished. Across the 157 self-extinguished tests with that measurement the median ratio is 0.72: the flame dies while about 38% of the fuel volume is still left.
  • The replay runs in real seconds next to a live d² chart that draws the five flight tests as reference curves, so the animation follows measured data. Flame size uses typical flame-to-droplet ratios from droplet theory and is labelled illustrative.

Solid materials: the limits measured in orbit

Fabrics and plastics are what burns in a spacecraft, and the challenge points to BASS, BASS-II and Saffire. From the BASS-II test matrices (PSI-25) we extracted the oxygen, CO and CO₂ readings of 129 tests, and from NASA's BASS-II summary report (NASA/TM-20210011385, also in PSI) the flammability boundary that the investigators fitted to their PMMA rod tests: XO₂ = 6·exp(−0.5a) + 0.0167a + 13.4 (%), where a = 1.5U/r is the stretch rate (p. 75, Eq. (6.1)).

  • The boundary bottoms out at about 13.5% O₂ near 1.7–2.5 cm/s of airflow, below the 15.4% limit of the normal-gravity upward test (p. 76 and p. 89). In the gentle airflow typical near spacecraft surfaces, solids burn at less oxygen than Earth tests indicate.
  • Rods needed about 14.5% O₂ to ignite (p. 68); once lit, flames survived to 13.5% (BASS-II table).
  • Near the limit, flames run fuel-rich: PMMA rods released a median 137 ppm of CO below 17% O₂ (23 tests) against 59 ppm above 20% (6 tests).
  • Three final-O₂ values in NASA's table (0.6–1.6%) are physically impossible in the 250 L glovebox; they are logged in the quality log and excluded.

The SI layer: briefings you can check

The challenge asks for a super intelligence (SI) powered dashboard. Skylab Phoenix uses SI where it is strong, turning evidence into plain language, and keeps it away from decisions: rankings, predictions and the out-of-data guard stay deterministic and tested.

11.1 How it works

  1. Evidence packs. For each of the 5 habitats and 24 investigations, pipeline/si.py writes numbered evidence items (E1, E2…) computed from the gold data: model predictions with their intervals, the most relevant investigations, coverage, validation and the top findings.
  2. SI drafts. An SI model (Anthropic Claude) writes a 2–4 sentence briefing from each pack. Each sentence lists the evidence items it relies on. scripts/si_generate.py regenerates the drafts through the Anthropic API.
  3. Verification firewall. Code publishes a briefing only if every sentence cites existing evidence, every number appears in the evidence it cites, nothing reads as a safety verdict (“is safe”, “certified”, “guaranteed”…), and the evidence is unchanged since the draft was written (hash). Otherwise the dashboard shows the evidence itself, labelled “Evidence brief”.

Result: 33 of 33 briefings pass. The firewall was exercised during development: correcting the EVA-prep pressure (10.3 → 10.2 psi) changed the evidence of 14 packs, and the firewall blocked exactly those 14 drafts, naming the outdated numbers, until they were rewritten. The test suite checks that invented numbers, wrong citations, uncited sentences, verdicts and stale drafts are all rejected.

Briefings cover the 5 habitats, the 24 investigations, the 3 lunar-base modules and the base as a whole. In Spanish, each briefing is a translation of the verified English text in which every number is copied automatically from the original, so translation cannot change a figure; quoted NASA passages are marked as translations.

Discussion and limitations

Saying clearly what the data does not allow is part of the result.

  • Droplets, not cabin materials. The main model was trained on methanol and heptane droplets. The defensible conclusion is that, in every fuel family NASA tested in μg, self-extinction disappears at ≥ 30% O₂. It is not a direct prediction for fabrics or polymers.
  • Little data near the Moon. 37 records in the lunar envelope and none of solids. The intervals reflect it and the out-of-data guard watches it.
  • CO₂ effect confounded with O₂. In FLEX, each CO₂ level was tested at a different O₂. We therefore show empirical rates and do not extrapolate a CO₂ dose.
  • BASS outcomes extracted from text. They come from regular expressions over operator notes and are hints for a combustion expert to review.
  • Solids concentrated in PMMA (177 of 238 tests). Other materials have very little data.
  • No partial gravity. PSI has no lunar or Martian data. Saffire-II bounds only the 20 cm/s regime.
  • Assumed conditions. In SPICE, SLICE, SAME, CLD and E-FIELD, O₂ and pressure are not reported per test and cabin air was assumed. They are flagged and down-weighted in the ranking.
  • Extrapolated limiting temperature. The 50% point in CFI-G lies above the hottest temperature tested.
  • Illustrations are labelled. Flame size in the Fire Lab and every partial-gravity flame are illustrations; module volumes of the lunar base are ISS and Orion stand-ins.
  • SI text is interpretation, not evidence. The firewall guarantees citations and numbers, not that an interpretation is the best one; the evidence behind every sentence is one click away.

What we did to mitigate it

Validation by whole test days, comparison with baselines, bootstrap intervals, a calibration diagram, an out-of-data guard with a per-input range check, a public correction log, a verified SI layer, automated tests in continuous integration, and wording that separates what was measured from what was extrapolated.

Impact for exploration and on Earth

  • Lunar habitat design. Quantifies how much the risk grows from cabin air to lunar-base air and what must be measured before certifying materials.
  • Experiment planning. The active-learning recommendation prioritizes tests for the ISS, LUCI or FM2 where they add the most information.
  • Detection and suppression. Flags cool flames as a risk for optical detectors, CO₂ and helium as suppressants with measurable effects, and ventilation as a design variable.
  • Open data engineering. The clean dataset and the PSI correction log serve any researcher.
  • On Earth. The same questions (oxygen-enriched atmospheres in hospitals, submarines and mines; smoke detection that tells smoke from dust; firefighter equipment) benefit from this data. The profiles include the Earth benefits PSI reports for each investigation.

Conclusions

  1. NASA's flight data shows that at ≥ 30% O₂ fire stops putting itself out: in FLEX, 2 of 24 droplets, and 0 of 17 at 0.70 atm.
  2. A simple, calibrated model (out-of-sample AUC 0.88) estimates that self-extinction falls from 72% in the cabin to 6% in the lunar base.
  3. The data gap is the most important finding for NASA: 37 records near lunar-base air, none of solids, none for the suit.
  4. The highest-value next test is CO₂ suppression at lunar-base O₂ and pressure.
  5. Everything is reproducible and traceable, and it is presented in a dashboard, with verified SI briefings, that says when it does not know.

Future work

  • A flammability envelope for solids (PMMA, SIBAL) with a Gaussian process in the flow–O₂ plane, after turning BASS flow sweeps into expert-reviewed points.
  • Extract results from PSI publications with an SI model and cross-check them against the tables with the same firewall.
  • Add partial-gravity data (LUCI, FM2) when PSI publishes it, and retrain.
  • A survival model for gas-flame burn time that treats flames still burning at fuel cut-off as censored.
