The Moon base will breathe 30% oxygen. Here is what NASA's flight data says about fire there — and where that data runs out.

Skylab Phoenix cleans every combustion experiment in NASA's Physical Sciences Informatics repository, profiles and ranks the 24 investigations for each crewed habitat, and runs validated models with explicit uncertainty — so the answer always comes with its evidence.

01 · COVERAGE

Where NASA has flown fire, and where crews will live

Every flight test with a known oxygen level and pressure, against the atmospheres of crewed habitats. The lunar-base envelope from the challenge brief (≥30% O₂, ≤10.2 psi) is almost empty, and no solid material has ever burned there in microgravity.

02 · INVESTIGATIONS

The 24 investigations, summarized and ranked per habitat

Each profile is generated from the data itself: what was varied, what was held fixed, what was measured, the conditions covered and the hardware. The ranking is a transparent formula, not a black box: closeness of the tested conditions to the habitat's atmosphere, volume of labeled results, and how directly the investigation targets crew fire safety.

06 · NEXT EXPERIMENT

What NASA should fly next

Active learning on the model: among conditions near the lunar-base atmosphere, Skylab Phoenix picks those where the 200 bootstrap models disagree the most — where one new test would teach the most. Partial-gravity data (LUCI, FM2) is not in PSI yet; these conditions are where it would pay off first.

07 · DATA & VALIDATION

Reproducible pipeline, honest validation

One command rebuilds everything from NASA's public API. Every correction to NASA's tables is logged, and the model is scored only on test days it never saw.

Model vs baselines

AUC: ranking quality (0.5 = chance). Brier and log loss: calibration of the probabilities (lower is better). The simpler logistic model beats gradient boosting on held-out days, so it is the one deployed.
