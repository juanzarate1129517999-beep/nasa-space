          01

          The question

          What does NASA's fire data say about the air crews will breathe?

          The challenge asks for fire-safety insight from NASA's microgravity combustion archive, with a lunar base planned at about 30% oxygen and 10.2 psi. We split that into three questions a safety engineer would ask, and every part of the site answers one of them.

          • Will a small fire put itself out?A model trained on FLEX droplet fires gives the chance, with an interval. Fire Lab.
          • Which NASA investigations matter most here?A transparent score ranks all 24 for each crew atmosphere. Ranking.
          • Where is the data missing?A coverage map, the base audit and the next-flight picks. Findings and Next flight.
          02

          Sources

          Only public NASA data, fetched by code.

          Nothing was typed in by hand. Scripts download each source and keep the original file, so any number can be traced back.

          03

          Cleaning

          We read every table and logged every fix.

          NASA's tables were exported from spreadsheets over fifteen years, and they carry the marks of it: placeholders typed into numeric columns, duplicated rows, units off by ten, a Greek μ that became “?”. Each rule below changed a value, and the log keeps the original next to the new one, so the change can be checked or undone. Nothing is silently dropped.

          04

          One table

          Nineteen experiments, one schema.

          Each experiment names its columns its own way. We map all of them to one table with SI units: oxygen as a mole fraction, pressure in atmospheres, flow in cm/s and one outcome field (went out on its own, kept burning, or not labelled). Two rules matter for honesty:

          • When a table does not report oxygen or pressure per test, we assume ISS cabin air and mark the record as assumed (hollow dots in every chart).
          • Only outcomes NASA recorded count as labels. We never infer an outcome from video or from other columns.
            05

            The model

            A simple model, tested on days it never saw.

            The self-extinction model is a logistic regression on six physical inputs: fuel, oxygen, pressure (log), CO₂, helium and droplet size. We chose it over gradient boosting because it scored better on held-out data and every coefficient can be read and checked against combustion physics: more oxygen, fewer fires that go out on their own.

            • Honest validation. Tests run on the same day share hardware and settings, so we hold out whole test days (GroupKFold, 5 folds). Random splits would score higher and mean less.
            • Uncertainty. 200 models are refit on bootstrap resamples of whole days; the interval on every prediction is their 5th to 95th percentile.
            • Knowing when not to answer. If the requested air is far from every flown test (mean distance to the 5 nearest beyond the 95th percentile of flown tests), the site says it is extrapolating; beyond twice that it withholds the number.
            • Physics in the Fire Lab. The droplet shrinks by the d² law with the burning rate and extinction diameter of the five closest FLEX tests, so the animation follows measured data, not a script.

            06

            Ranking

            Three criteria you can see and change.

            Each investigation gets a relevance score out of 100 for each crew atmosphere. The score adds three parts, with default weights you can move on the Ranking page:

            50%Atmosphere closenessHow many of its tests sit near the habitat's oxygen and pressure, weighted by distance.
            20%Data volumeHow many records with a labelled outcome it contributes (log scale).
            30%Fire-safety directnessWhether it measured prevention, detection or suppression directly, from NASA's own descriptions.

            To check the order does not hinge on our weights, we re-rank with 2,000 random weightings and report how often each investigation keeps its place.

            07

            SI with a firewall

            The SI writes; code decides what gets published.

            The briefings are drafted by an SI model (Anthropic Claude) from an evidence pack the pipeline computes. The model never sees the raw data and never decides anything: rankings, predictions and the out-of-data guard are plain code. Before a briefing is shown, code checks every sentence:

            1. It cites at least one evidence item that exists.
            2. Every number in it appears in the items it cites.
            3. It makes no safety verdict (“is safe”, “certified”, “guaranteed”).
            4. The evidence pack has not changed since the draft was written.

            If any check fails, the site shows the evidence itself, labelled “Evidence brief”. In Spanish, briefings are translated from the verified English text and their numbers are copied from it automatically, so translation cannot change a figure.

            08

            Limits

            What this work cannot tell you.

            • The model covers fuel droplets only, because FLEX is the only investigation with enough labelled outcomes. Solids are described from BASS, BASS-II and Saffire, not predicted.
            • No combustion test in PSI ran at lunar or Martian gravity. Every partial-gravity flame on this site is an illustration.
            • No solid in NASA's PSI tables burned above 22.7% oxygen. NASA's Saffire V and VI burned four large samples at 26–31% oxygen and 0.53–0.70 atm (Urban et al., ICES-2024-365); the site shows them as published data, outside PSI. They show that fabric and acrylic burn there, not where they stop burning, so every materials limit for 30% air is still an extrapolation and is labelled so.
            • Module volumes for the Moon base use ISS and Orion volumes as stand-ins.
            • This is a research aid built for the challenge. It is not flight certification and is not endorsed by NASA.
